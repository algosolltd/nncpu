\title{The Gate, Not the Network?\\
A Matched-Control Audit of Learned Cache Prefetching}
\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Simone Jarno Casartelli and Enrico Lopedoto}
\IEEEauthorblockA{\textit{Algorithmica Solutions}\\
\texttt{simone@algorithmicasolutions.com},
\texttt{enrico@algorithmicasolutions.com}}}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Learned cache prefetchers are often evaluated against classical predictors
that always issue requests. This confounds address prediction with admission:
a learned system can appear safer simply because it declines to prefetch.
We audit a streaming MLP delta predictor using matched classical controls,
each receiving the same confidence policy, and a simulator that accounts for
prefetch completion latency, set associativity, issue bandwidth, finite
prefetch MSHRs, lookahead, useful coverage, late requests, and pollution.

The original neural interpretation does not survive the matched comparison.
On random traffic the MLP and gated stride are statistically indistinguishable;
on a timed strided stream the MLP requires approximately $1.96\times$ as many
cycles as a matched non-neural control. It has a conditional $1.101\times$
advantage on one KV-cache pattern, but no general lead. This negative result
motivates a predictor-independent alternative: admit requests when the
dominant delta occupies at least 35\% of a 16-access window. Parameters are
selected on seeds 0--9 and evaluated on holdout seeds 10--39. In a
bandwidth/MSHR-constrained profile, the filter improves stride by
$1.088\times$ on random and $1.076\times$ on phased traffic while removing
99.55\% and 83.07\% of accepted prefetch traffic. It leaves selected regular
controls unchanged but has explicit counterexamples. We present a reproducible
negative result and a promising admission control, not a hardware claim.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
An inaccurate prefetch consumes bandwidth and cache capacity. Confidence can
reduce this cost by suppressing requests on unpredictable streams. A learned
mechanism naturally exposes a confidence signal, but comparing it with an
always-on stride prefetcher changes prediction and admission simultaneously.
Their combined effect cannot be attributed to the neural network.

This paper began as a positive study of a 6--32--1 online MLP that predicts
the next address delta. An audit found that the largest speedups assumed
instantaneous prefetch completion and that the decisive random-stream result
lacked a classical predictor with the same gate. We make those failures the
research question:

\begin{quote}
What remains of a learned-prefetching result after admission, timeliness,
bandwidth, lookahead, cache organization, and statistical unit are matched?
\end{quote}

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
\item a matched-control evaluation separating the MLP from its gate;
\item a latency- and contention-aware simulator with a demand-only shadow L1;
\item a 16-entry dominant-delta admission filter tuned on seeds 0--9 and
evaluated on 30 disjoint holdout seeds;
\item machine-verifiable raw results, claims, hashes, and statistics that
collapse repeated deterministic traces into one experimental unit.
\end{itemize}

The principal finding is negative: the evidence does not support a general
neural advantage. The constructive result is narrower: admission based on
recent delta regularity removes most useless traffic without the large
false-negative cost of the original prediction-error gate.

\section{Machine and measurement model}
\nncpu{} executes an in-order scalar instruction stream. Fetch costs one
cycle; an L1 hit costs one and a demand miss has 40-cycle memory latency. The
L1 holds 32 lines of eight 64-bit words under LRU. The ideal profile is fully
associative and completes prefetches immediately; it is retained only as an
upper-bound control.

Timed profiles use an 8-way L1 and asynchronous 40-cycle prefetches. A demand
that catches an in-flight request waits for its remaining latency. The
\emph{timed} profile starts one memory request per cycle and has 16 prefetch
MSHRs. The \emph{constrained} profile starts one request every four cycles and
has eight. Demand and prefetches share the issue schedule. Excess requests are
recorded as dropped rather than counted as memory traffic.

A same-geometry shadow L1 is updated only by demand accesses. Comparing actual
and shadow outcomes identifies useful hits (actual hit, shadow miss), pollution
misses (actual miss, shadow hit), late prefetches, redundant requests, and
MSHR drops. These measurements are local to the declared model; they do not
replace an out-of-order cycle-accurate simulator.

\section{Predictors and matched admission}
Global stride predicts the previous delta. A bounded delta-Markov control
records which delta most often follows the current delta. These are controlled
models rather than faithful reproductions of published hardware. The learned
predictor is an online 6--32--1 ReLU MLP trained on a 64-sample replay buffer.
It receives opcode, static PC when present, address, two deltas, and a bias;
its next-delta target is clamped to $[-16,16]$ words.

All mechanisms expose one next-address prediction. A predictor-independent
adapter extrapolates its delta by lookahead distance $D$, keeping prediction,
admission, and latency coverage as separate mechanisms.

\subsection{Matched prediction-error gate}
The original gate admits a request when median recent prediction error is no
greater than half the characteristic delta magnitude over 48 accesses. We
apply the identical gate to stride and delta-Markov. Wrapped predictors keep
updating while requests are suppressed, allowing phase recovery. This control
isolates what the MLP contributes beyond admission.

\subsection{Dominant-delta regularity gate}
Prediction error rejects useful multimodal streams. We instead define recent
regularity
\begin{equation}
 R_t=\frac{\max_d |\{i:d_i=d\}|}{W}.
\end{equation}
After an eight-access warm-up, the filter admits a request iff
$R_t\ge0.35$ for $W=16$. It stores 16 signed deltas plus a small frequency
table, requires no predictor output, and continues updating the wrapped
predictor while closed. Window and threshold were chosen on development seeds
0--9; every confirmatory number uses seeds 10--39.

\section{Experimental design}
We evaluate five synthetic streams, four controlled ML/agent patterns, and
three instrumented algorithm traces. The latter record conceptual word
accesses from binary search, matrix multiplication, and merge sort; they are
not native SPEC/GAP traces. Every stochastic workload is materialized once per
seed and shared byte-for-byte by all configurations.

The ideal profile uses $D=1$. Predeclared timed and constrained points use
$D=16$ and $D=8$; the artifact also retains the complete
$D\in\{1,8,16,32\}$ sensitivity sweep. We report paired cycle ratio
\begin{equation}
 B(A,C)=\operatorname{mean}_s C_s/A_s,
\end{equation}
so $B>1$ favors candidate $A$. Exact two-sided sign tests discard ties and
receive one Holm correction across the saved comparison family.

A SHA-256 identifies each materialized stream. Repeated deterministic
classical runs on the same hash count as one experimental unit; distinct
stochastic streams and MLP seeds remain separate. This prevents repeated
traces from creating false statistical precision.

independently varied agent-RAG streams.

same filter to delta-Markov yields similar effects, so it is not stride-only.

\subsection{Counterexamples and sensitivity}
The filter is not a no-harm theorem. At ideal $D=1$ it slows agent RAG by
1.30\%. On the single deterministic merge-sort trace at $D=1$, filtered
stride is 5.95\% slower. That is one descriptive trace, not 30 independent
observations. Larger lookahead reverses the same trace. Binary search also
changes sign across predictor and distance.

This sensitivity is fundamental: an accurate request may arrive late, exceed
MSHR capacity, or compete with demand. The artifact retains all distances and
states the selected point instead of choosing the best row per workload.

\section{Threats to validity}
The main limitation is external validity. \nncpu{} is a deterministic
latency-accounting in-order model, not ChampSim, gem5, or silicon. It omits an
out-of-order window, TLBs, coherence, multiple cache levels, request
prioritization, and predictor area/energy/critical-path cost. Classical
predictors are abstractions. Algorithm traces are instrumented address
sequences, while ML/agent streams are workload-shaped models.

Internal validity is stronger: configurations are paired on exact stream
hashes; demand and prefetch contend; MSHR drops, lateness, shadow coverage,
and pollution are distinct; tuning and holdout seeds do not overlap; every
table value is checked from raw rows. These properties justify claims about
the declared model only.

The next evidence gate is native ChampSim integration with public traces,
followed by SPEC/GAP where licensing permits, hardware-cost synthesis, and
official baseline implementations. Until then, this is suitable as a
reproducibility/negative-results or architecture-workshop study, not proof of
deployable speedup.

\section{Reproducibility}
The holdout artifact contains 21,240 raw rows under
\texttt{results/research\_gate\_validation}. It records the generating
commit, tracked-tree cleanliness, source/configuration SHA-256, raw rows,
aggregates, contrasts, independent-unit counts, sign tests, and Holm
adjustments. Machine-readable claims are in
\texttt{paper/research\_claims.json}. Run
\begin{verbatim}
python -m benchmarks.verify_research
\end{verbatim}
to recompute summaries and contrasts and check provenance and claims.

\section{Conclusion}
A matched control changes the interpretation of this learned-prefetching
study. The MLP is neither generally safer nor generally faster; its convincing
random-stream behavior is reproduced by a classical predictor with the same
admission policy. The defensible contribution is smaller: recent delta
regularity is an inexpensive, predictor-independent signal that removes most
harmful random traffic and improves phased traffic on disjoint holdout seeds.
It also has documented counterexamples. This is an audit of attribution and a
candidate admission mechanism; hardware validation remains required.
